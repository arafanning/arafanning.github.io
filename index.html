About

How I approach clinical and research questions

In neuropsychological assessment, I have worked with patients whose cognitive concerns arise alongside epilepsy, movement disorders, brain injury, and other complicated medical histories. Interpreting performance requires more than identifying strengths and weaknesses. Neurologic findings, imaging, psychiatric and developmental factors, and everyday functioning all bear on what a cognitive pattern might mean for an individual patient.

I bring that same attention to interpretation into my research. Decisions about how to characterize cannabis use, construct cognitive scores, or define functional difficulty can change the comparisons being made, sometimes before a model is ever fitted. I hope to pursue these questions in an academic medical center, where clinical work continually tests the usefulness and limits of what we know from research.

Research

Substance use, neurocognition, and everyday life

I am interested in how a research finding changes with the comparison being made: who is included, how substance use is described, and whether the outcome is cognitive performance, a specific ability, or functioning outside the assessment room.

01

HIV, cannabis, and neurocognition

My neuroHIV work examines cannabis use in relation to overall and domain-specific cognition, changes in activities of daily living, and medication adherence. HIV disease severity is considered alongside these outcomes, not assumed to explain them.

02

Adolescent substance use

Through the ABCD Study®, I contribute to longitudinal research on adolescent substance use and development, including work involving mental health, school discipline, and the social settings in which young people grow up.

03

Measurement and quantitative methods

I work primarily in R, using regression, robust standard errors, planned contrasts, and latent-variable methods. I pay close attention to score construction, whether comparisons are supported by the available data, and what an estimate can reasonably mean beyond its statistical significance.

Dissertation research

Understanding the Effect of Cannabis on the Neurocognitive Health of People Living With HIV

The dissertation begins with a familiar comparison between people who use cannabis and those who do not, considered alongside HIV status. That distinction is useful, but incomplete. It does not tell us whether associations differ with amount of use, whether they appear in overall cognition or particular domains, or what they mean beyond an assessment setting.

The first study examined HIV and cannabis together, comparing average neurocognitive performance and domain-specific impairment. The next focuses on people living with HIV and asks whether cognition differs across levels of past-year cannabis use, while considering demographic characteristics and HIV disease severity. Rather than assume that one cognitive summary captures the full picture, the work asks what each outcome allows us to see.

The third study moves into everyday functioning. Changes in activities of daily living, current difficulties, and medication adherence are considered separately because they represent different aspects of life and do not necessarily correspond to performance on standardized tests. A fourth study, still planned, will examine inflammatory biomarkers in relation to cognitive and functional outcomes.

What connects these studies is a concern with interpretation. A difference observed in a statistical model is not automatically a difference that changes how someone manages daily life, just as the absence of an average group difference does not mean that individual patterns are unimportant. Both require closer examination.

Research repositories: Published HIV and cannabis study, Neurocognitive analyses, and Everyday functioning analyses. Documentation is being revised; participant-level data are not publicly available.
