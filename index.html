About

How I approach clinical and research questions

In neuropsychological assessment, I have worked with patients whose cognitive concerns arise alongside epilepsy, movement disorders, brain injury, and other complicated medical histories. Interpreting performance requires more than identifying strengths and weaknesses. Neurologic findings, imaging, psychiatric and developmental factors, and everyday functioning all bear on what a cognitive pattern might mean for an individual patient.

I bring that same attention to interpretation into my research. Decisions about how to characterize cannabis use, construct cognitive scores, or define functional difficulty can change the comparisons being made, sometimes before a model is ever fitted. I hope to pursue these questions in an academic medical center, where clinical work continually tests the usefulness and limits of what we know from research.

Research

Substance use, neurocognition, and everyday life

I am interested in how a research finding changes with the comparison being made: who is included, how substance use is described, and whether the outcome is cognitive performance, a specific ability, or functioning outside the assessment room.

01

HIV, cannabis, and neurocognition

My neuroHIV work examines cannabis use in relation to overall and domain-specific cognition, changes in activities of daily living, and medication adherence. HIV disease severity is considered alongside these outcomes, not assumed to explain them.

02

Adolescent substance use

Through the ABCD Study®, I contribute to longitudinal research on adolescent substance use and development, including work involving mental health, school discipline, and the social settings in which young people grow up.

03

Measurement and quantitative methods

I work primarily in R, using regression, robust standard errors, planned contrasts, and latent-variable methods. I pay close attention to score construction, whether comparisons are supported by the available data, and what an estimate can reasonably mean beyond its statistical significance.

Dissertation research

Understanding the Effect of Cannabis on the Neurocognitive Health of People Living With HIV

Whether cannabis use is associated with poorer neurocognitive health among people living with HIV has never had one consistent answer. Studies have pointed in different directions, depending on the amount and history of use, HIV status, and the cognitive abilities examined (Thames et al., 2016; Crook et al., 2021; Ayoub et al., 2024). My dissertation approaches those differences by asking a narrower question: for whom, under what conditions, and in which aspects of cognition or daily life do cannabis-related associations emerge?

Across three connected studies, I move from comparisons defined by HIV and cannabis status to differences across amounts of cannabis use among people living with HIV, and finally to functioning outside the assessment setting. The picture changes as the question becomes more specific. Average cognitive performance does not necessarily reveal the same differences as domain-specific impairment, and distinctions between lower and higher amounts of use become difficult to see when all cannabis users are treated as one group. HIV disease severity provides important clinical context, but it does not, on its own, account for these patterns. Nor do the results establish a cognitive benefit of using smaller amounts.

The final study turns to everyday functioning without assuming that neurocognitive test performance captures what happens outside the assessment setting. Here, too, the outcome matters. Reported medication adherence emerges as a more specific concern than a broad decline in activities of daily living or performance on a structured medication-management task. Adherence has been examined as a distinct concern in other neuroHIV research (Manuzak et al., 2023), although self-reported adherence, observed skills, and actual daily routines should not be treated as interchangeable. Taken together, this work argues against treating cannabis use or neurocognitive health as a single, uniform phenomenon. The question is not simply whether an association exists. It is who is represented by that association, when it appears, and what it can reasonably tell us.

Beyond the dissertation

The dissertation concludes with the everyday-functioning and medication-adherence study. After my defense, I may extend this research through a separate publication examining inflammatory biomarkers in relation to cognition and everyday functioning. That work remains a future direction, not part of the dissertation.
